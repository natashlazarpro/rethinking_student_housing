# 5.2 Reflection 

**Theoretical Reflection** 

The results confirm the relevance of the chosen theoretical framework and demonstrate that the student housing crisis in Berlin cannot be explained within the framework of a single disciplinary perspective. The combination of Central Place Theory, Right to the City Theory and Affordability Theory made it possible to interpret the empirical data as a manifestation of structural spatial, social and economic contradictions in the urban system.

The application of Central Place Theory showed that universities do indeed function as central places, forming stable zones of demand attraction. The identified polycentricity of Berlin's educational space confirms the classic theory of a multi-level hierarchy of centres: the most central areas accumulate the maximum student demand, while the peripheral zones perform supporting functions. At the same time, empirical results refine the theory, demonstrating that in conditions of acute housing shortage, pressure is formed not by individual central places, but by the overlap of several zones of accessibility, which intensifies competition and leads to systemic market overheating. Thus, Central Place Theory allows us not only to describe the spatial structure of demand, but also to identify the mechanisms of its concentration.

Right to the City Theory provides a critical framework for interpreting the spatial inequalities identified. The concentration of private student residences and private universities in central and prestigious areas reflects the logic of capitalising on urban space, where access to housing is determined by ability to pay rather than social need. The even distribution of state-owned halls of residence on the outskirts, on the one hand, indicates the state's attempt to provide affordable housing, but on the other hand, it effectively reinforces the spatial displacement of students from the central urban fabric. This contradiction directly correlates with Lefebvre's ideas about the city as a social product and emphasises that students' right to the city in Berlin is realised in a fragmented and uneven manner.

Affordability Theory allows us to supplement spatial analysis with a social interpretation of students' behavioural strategies. A high subjective assessment of rental affordability, coupled with acute shortages and high pressure, indicates not real affordability, but rather students' forced adaptation to limited choices. Empirical data confirm Stone's thesis that housing affordability cannot be reduced to the level of rent. Students sacrifice proximity to the university, housing quality, and social integration in order to reduce their financial burden, which creates a gap between their places of study and residence and exacerbates spatial segregation.

The combined application of these three theories allows us to view the student housing crisis as a structural systemic phenomenon rather than the result of local imbalances. The spatial organisation of educational centres, the market logic of development, and the limited financial resources of students reinforce each other, forming a stable model of unequal access to housing. Thus, the theoretical framework confirms that solving the student housing crisis in Berlin is impossible without simultaneously revising spatial planning, the city's social responsibility, and the principles of assessing housing affordability. 

The theories used in this work not only explain the empirical patterns identified, but also provide a critical basis for the formation of a more equitable and sustainable housing policy for students.


**Methodological Reflection** 

Despite the use of several research methods in various chapters of the work, spatial analysis is the key methodological tool of this study. Unlike exclusively quantitative or qualitative approaches, spatial analysis allows us to answer not only the questions of what, why, and how, but also a question that is fundamentally important for urban planning: where are the structural imbalances in the student housing market formed and manifested?

More than ten different spatial analysis techniques were used in the preparation of this thesis. However, not all of them demonstrated statistically significant differences or led to fundamentally new interpretations. For example, a gravity model was developed as part of the study using mathematical methods and elements of machine learning, but the results obtained largely reproduced the spatial axes of urban development previously identified using Central Place Analysis. This confirmed the stability of the identified spatial patterns, but also justified the choice in favour of less complex and more interpretable analytical tools.

A key methodological decision was to use automated analysis procedures based on the Python programming language, as well as to deliberately limit the study to two key sets of geodata: student accommodation capacity and higher education university student demand. This approach ensured the reproducibility of the study: with similar data available, the methodology can be reapplied both in Berlin in 3–5 years to analyse market dynamics and in other large cities.

During the work, the input data was repeatedly supplemented and refined, which led to changes in the preliminary and final results. The use of programmable analytical processes made it possible to quickly update calculations and visualisations. Without automation, each new entry would have required repeating a significant amount of manual operations in geographic information systems such as QGIS or ArcGIS, which would have significantly increased the risk of errors and reduced the reproducibility of the analysis.

It should also be noted that classical spatial analysis typically uses more than two sets of geodata. To form a more comprehensive picture, it would be advisable to compare the results obtained with data on transport accessibility, population density, the type of buildings, and other spatial indicators. However, the inclusion of such comparisons goes beyond the scientific objectives formulated at the beginning of the study and was therefore deliberately not implemented in this work.

It should also be emphasised that the deliberate limitation of the amount of input data was a methodological decision and not an oversight. Focusing on two key geodata sets — student accommodation capacity and higher education university student demand — made it possible to avoid excessive model complexity and maintain the interpretability of the results. Given the high heterogeneity of the data and the lack of uniform standards for accounting for student housing in Berlin, the inclusion of additional layers could have increased noise and made it more difficult to identify structural patterns.

The use of a mixed approach combining quantitative spatial analysis with qualitative methods made it possible to compensate for the limitations of each method individually. Spatial analysis identified areas of increased market pressure and structural imbalances between supply and demand, while qualitative data allowed these patterns to be interpreted through the experiences of students, their everyday practices and strategies for adapting to the crisis. Thus, the research methodology goes beyond purely descriptive mapping and aims to identify the socio-spatial consequences of the housing crisis.

It should be noted that the results of spatial analysis depend to a large extent on the quality and completeness of the source data. In particular, data on the capacity of student dormitories and the number of students per university are aggregated and do not take into account factors such as actual turnover, short-term rentals, informal forms of accommodation, and illegal sublets. Qualitative research has shown that these forms of housing play a significant role in the actual housing experience of students, but remain ‘invisible’ in official statistics.

Finally, the methodological approach of this study should be viewed as a basis for further research rather than a complete model. In the future, spatial analysis could be expanded to include transport accessibility, travel time costs, rental price gradients, and regulatory constraints. Nevertheless, even within the chosen limitations, the study demonstrates that combining reproducible, automated spatial methods with socially oriented analysis not only allows us to diagnose the student housing crisis, but also lays the foundation for informed policy and planning decisions.

**Empirical Reflection**

The empirical part of the study showed that quantitative spatial indicators are highly accurate but require careful interpretation without automatically transferring them to social reality. Spatial indices of pressure, accessibility, and concentration allow us to identify risk areas and structural inconsistencies, but they do not in themselves reflect the subjective experience of living, informal practices, and institutional barriers faced by students. This highlights the gap between the “measurable” accessibility of housing and its actual suitability for students' everyday lives.

The empirical approach is particularly valuable because it combines automated spatial analysis with qualitative data, which has revealed a discrepancy between the formal logic of student housing allocation and students' actual adaptation strategies. Interviews and surveys showed that many student decisions are not rational choices in the classical economic sense, but rather forced compromises in conditions of limited choice. This points to the need for cautious use of behavioural assumptions in accessibility and demand models.

In the context of future research, the results of this study open up several avenues for further empirical exploration. A promising direction is the inclusion of a time dimension, allowing for the analysis of changes in pressure and affordability over time, as well as the integration of data on transport connectivity, contract types, and socio-legal aspects of housing. In addition, the proposed methodology can be applied in comparative studies of other university cities, which will allow us to identify both universal and context-specific mechanisms of student housing pressure.
